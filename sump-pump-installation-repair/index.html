Basement taking on water: what to do now

Water in the basement can reach outlets and appliances quickly. Do not wade into standing water if the electrical panel or outlets are underwater. Cut power at the main panel if you can reach it from dry ground, and call the dispatcher.

While you wait

  • ✓Check that the pump is plugged in and the breaker has not tripped
  • ✓Look for debris in the pit or a float stuck against the wall
  • ✓Pour a bucket of water into the pit to see if the pump starts
  • ✓Move belongings off the floor
  • ✓Do not use extension cords across wet floors

How a sump pump works and why it fails

A sump pit collects groundwater from drain tile around the foundation. A float switch starts the pump when the water rises, and the pump sends it through a discharge pipe to the yard. Failure usually comes from a stuck float, a burned-out motor, a clogged intake or a power outage during a storm.

New pits and backup systems

Homes without a pit may take in water through the floor slab or at the wall joint. A plumber breaks out a section of slab, sets a basin and connects the drain tile. A backup system adds a second pump on a battery, so the basement stays dry when a storm knocks out power.

Where the water goes matters. The discharge line should run away from the foundation and not toward a neighbor's yard. Many cities regulate it, so check with Ballwin Building Inspections at (636) 227-2129 for local rules. For permanent seepage, see basement waterproofing.

Sump pump or sewer backup

A flooded basement is not always groundwater. If the water comes from a floor drain and smells foul, it is a sewer backup, which needs sewer line cleaning and not a pump. Tell the dispatcher what the water looks like and where it appears.
