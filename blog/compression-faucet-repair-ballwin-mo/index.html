Compression Faucet Repair: A Ballwin, MO Homeowner Guide

How to fix a dripping two-handle compression faucet, and when the old fixture should go.

Two handles that you tighten down to stop the water usually mean a compression faucet. It is the oldest common faucet design, and the one that drips most often. The cause is rarely a mystery, and the repair is within reach of a careful homeowner.

This guide walks through how the faucet works, how to replace the washer and seat and when a new faucet is the better answer. The faucet repair page covers other types, and we handle both cases when you call.

How a compression faucet works

Turning the handle drives a stem down so a rubber washer presses against a brass seat inside the valve body. That squeeze stops the flow. When the washer hardens or the seat roughens, water slips by and the faucet drips, even when closed tight.

Step by step: replacing the washer and seat

  1. 01

    Shut off the water

    Close the stop valve under the sink for that faucet, then open the faucet to drain it.

  2. 02

    Remove the handle

    Pop off the cap, take out the screw and pull the handle off.

  3. 03

    Unscrew the stem

    Use a wrench on the packing nut, then lift out the stem.

  4. 04

    Replace the washer

    Remove the screw at the stem bottom, install a matching washer and screw, and replace the packing.

  5. 05

    Check the seat

    Run a finger around the brass seat. If it is rough, resurface it with a seat dressing tool or replace it.

  6. 06

    Reassemble and test

    Put it back together, open the stop valve slowly and check for leaks.

Take the old washer to a hardware store and match it by size and shape. Washers come in flat and beveled forms, and the wrong shape will not seal.

Common mistakes

Avoid these

  • ✓Overtightening the handle to stop a drip, which wears the washer faster
  • ✓Forcing a frozen stem with a pipe wrench and cracking the body
  • ✓Skipping the seat check and putting a new washer on a rough seat
  • ✓Reassembling without replacing the packing or O-ring
  • ✓Opening the stop valve fast and shocking the lines

Repair or replace the faucet

A compression faucet with corroded stems, a pitted body or discontinued parts is a candidate for replacement. A modern cartridge or ceramic disc faucet seals without washers and lasts longer between repairs. If stop valves under the sink are old and seized, replace them at the same time.

Bathroom or kitchen updates often include a new faucet and fixtures. See plumbing installation for the other jobs that go together.

Choosing replacement parts and tools

Gather the tools before you start. You will need an adjustable wrench, a screwdriver, a small flashlight, a rag to protect the finish and a container for small parts. A seat wrench helps if the seat needs to come out. Lay the parts on a towel in the order you remove them so reassembly goes smoothly.

Washers vary in size and shape, and a repair kit with an assortment is often cheaper than a single washer. Choose rubber washers for general use and consider a brass or stainless screw to resist corrosion. If the stem threads look worn, replace the stem as well.

Finish by running water for a minute with the aerator removed, which flushes any debris loosened during the repair. Then replace the aerator and check the handle for smooth action. A faucet that still drips after a careful repair has probably reached the end of its useful life.

When a compression faucet is the wrong thing to keep

Compression faucets are cheap to repair, but repairs add up. If you replace washers every year, the seats are probably worn and the stems are loosening in their threads. At that point a modern faucet saves time and water.

Modern faucets also include aerators that limit flow, and many use ceramic discs that rarely drip. If you are updating a kitchen or bath, ask a plumber to bring a couple of faucet options and compare the price of a new unit against another round of parts and labor.

The short version

If the drip comes from the spout, replace the washer and check the seat. If it comes from the handle, replace the packing or O-ring. Shut off the stop valve first, keep track of every part you remove and test slowly. When the stem is stuck, the body is cracked or you cannot stop the water at the stop valve, put the wrench down and call a licensed plumber before a small drip becomes a flooded cabinet.
